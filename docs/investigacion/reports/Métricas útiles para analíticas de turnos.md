# Measure coverage per slot, fairness per person

The most valuable additions are not more counts of bad days. They are **hours-weighted coverage measured per 30-minute slot**, **fairness measured per person, normalised by contract and judged over rolling months**, **cost in premium equivalent hours priced at the rate in force on each date**, and a small set of **planning-process KPIs taken from solver runs and the change log**. The dashboard already flags days with deficit or excess. What it cannot yet show is how many person-hours are missing and where in the day the gaps recur. Vendors (Infor, Dayforce, Dialpad, UKG) and NHS safe-staffing practice all answer that question with one coverage %, one efficiency or over-coverage %, under-hours, over-hours and a weekday × time-slot heatmap of net gap. On fairness, the existing max−min spread depends on just two people. Head-to-head nurse-rostering experiments found that minimising each person's deviation from the team mean produced the fairest rosters, so per-person deviation bars and mean absolute deviation should come next. Colombia's labour reform makes cost tracking urgent. The Sunday/holiday surcharge rose to 90% on 1 July 2026 and reaches 100% on 1 July 2027, and the night window has started at 19:00 since 25 December 2025. A salary-free "premium equivalent hours" metric, with dated rates, separates the volume effect from the rate effect and projects the 2027 step. Be honest about the evidence: outcome links are good for understaffing (nursing mortality) and for overtime (burnout), only directional for preference fulfilment, and limited to vendor practice for any particular chart. No peer-reviewed study shows that one visualisation leads to better rostering decisions.

## Slot-level coverage replaces "deficit days" as the core measure

Day-level flags hide two things: how large the gap is, and gaps that cancel out within the day. Infor warns that surplus in one period can mask a shortfall in another, so a day looks fully covered when it is not ([Infor](https://docs.infor.com/wfm/2026.10/en-us/wfmopolh/mvs_legacy_user_on-premise/dvu1475661991644.html)). The fix is to compute the gap per slot and per position before aggregating. Infor publishes the only exact vendor formulas: **Coverage = 1 − (Total Understaffed ÷ Interval Requirement)** and **Efficiency = 1 − ((Total Overstaffed + Total Understaffed) ÷ Interval Requirement)**. Its worked example has a requirement of 36, 9 under and 9 over, giving 75% coverage and 50% efficiency ([Infor WFM](https://docs.infor.com/wfm/2026/en-us/wfmclolh/reporting_birst_user_cloud/lnj1614975615197.html)). Dayforce gives a "Coverage Score" where 100% means the schedule matches requirements at every point of the day, but it does not publish the formula ([Dayforce](https://help.dayforce.com/r/manager-guide/Coverage-Scores)). The app splits demand into mandatory (M) and desirable (D). That maps directly onto the minimum/maximum coverage band used in nurse-rostering research, where going outside the band is penalised as a soft-constraint violation ([Burke et al. 2004](https://alexandrechecoli.github.io/1-meta/Materiais/Artigos/2004%20-%20The%20State%20of%20the%20Art%20of%20Nurse%20Rostering%20-%20Burke,%20Causmaecker.pdf)). Below M is a deficit, M to D is acceptable, and above D is excess.

The evidence for monitoring understaffing comes from nursing, not support services. In Griffiths et al., **each day a patient spent with RN staffing below the ward mean raised the hazard of death by 3%** (aHR 1.03) ([Griffiths et al. 2019](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/141740/1/bmjqs_2018_008043.full.pdf)). That justifies counting exposure days rather than averaging them away. Saville et al. used **"% of shifts more than 15% short"** as their primary outcome. Staffing to mean demand left 31.8% of shifts understaffed, and the authors recommend baseline capacity near the **90th percentile of demand** ([Saville et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7788209)). NICE treats "red flags", such as fewer than two registered nurses on a shift, as triggers for immediate escalation rather than as averages ([NHS England safe staffing appendices](https://www.england.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/safe-staffing-adult-in-patient-appendices.pdf)). NHS trusts publish fill rate (actual ÷ planned hours) split day/night ([FHFT](https://www.fhft.nhs.uk/about-us/how-we-are-run/our-performance/nursing-and-midwifery-safe-staffing-inpatient-bedded-area)) with RAG bands. One trust uses green 90–110%, amber 80–<90%, red <80% and blue >110%, and trusts set their own thresholds ([Sherwood Forest](https://www.sfh-tr.nhs.uk/media/2475/enc-11-safe-staffing-appendix-2-description-of-tha-data-monitoring-colour-codes.pdf)). No support-service or Colombian staffing standard turned up, so treat every threshold below as local policy.

Notation: s is a 30-minute slot, p a position, f a time band (franja: morning, afternoon, night), st(s,p) the number of rostered people covering the slot, and M and D the mandatory and desirable demand. All hours are person-hours, so each slot contributes × 0.5 h.

| Metric | Exact definition | Chart |
|---|---|---|
| Under-hours (mandatory) | Σ_s,p max(0, M − st) × 0.5 | KPI tile; monthly bar per position |
| Over-hours (vs desirable) | Σ_s,p max(0, st − D) × 0.5 | KPI tile; monthly bar |
| Desirable gap hours | Σ max(0, D − st) × 0.5, over slots where st ≥ M | Secondary tile (not a safety issue) |
| Coverage % (Infor) | 1 − Σunder ÷ ΣM | KPI tile + monthly trend line |
| Efficiency % (Infor) | 1 − (Σunder + Σover) ÷ ΣM | KPI tile + monthly trend line |
| Slot coverage % | #slots with st ≥ M ÷ #slots with M > 0 | KPI tile |
| Fill rate by franja | Σ scheduled hours ÷ Σ M hours, per franja-day | Calendar heatmap (day × franja), RAG colours |
| % franjas badly short | #franja-days with Σunder ÷ ΣM > 15% ÷ #franja-days | KPI tile + trend |
| Red-flag slots | #slots with st < M, or st below an absolute floor (e.g. <2 on night) | Count tile; list for escalation |
| Margin distribution | histogram of st − M over all slots with M > 0 | Histogram; the bar at 0 shows fragility |
| P90 capacity check | st(s) vs P90 of M over the same weekday and slot | Line overlay on the daily chart |

Two charts carry most of the value. The first is a **weekday × 30-minute slot heatmap of the mean net gap** (st − M, with a toggle to st − D) on a diverging palette: red for shortfall, blue for surplus, grey for in band. It is the common pattern across Dialpad ([Dialpad](https://help.dialpad.com/docs/using-the-insights-report.md)), ServiceNow ([ServiceNow](https://www.servicenow.com/docs/r/t~YvqQkg2oQX4L7ySrpyuQ/FRtoZQYnL35PSXnq_yhzcA)) and Timefold's hour × weekday "shift efficiency" grid ([Timefold](https://docs.timefold.ai/employee-shift-scheduling/1.31.x/user-guide/visualizations)). It shows structural problems such as "every Monday 06:00–07:00 is short", which a list of deficit days cannot. The second is a **daily step chart** for a selected risk day, with M and D drawn as a band and staffed drawn as a step area, the format Snap Schedule uses ([Snap Schedule](https://www.snapschedule.com/docs/ss365/scheduler-manual/Scheduling_Your_Employees/Scheduler-Dashboard.html)). It explains why a day was flagged. Both are justified by vendor practice, not by experiments.

## Fairness needs per-person deviations, FTE normalisation and rolling windows

Max−min shows the extremes and ignores the other 38 people. Smet and Vanden Berghe showed that weighted-sum roster scoring "far from guarantees a fair solution", because one person's very bad roster is offset by others' good ones ([Smet & Vanden Berghe](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/176620)). Martin et al. compared fairness objectives over per-nurse penalties. **Minimising deviation from the mean (MinDev) was best on average, minimax was a close second, and the plain weighted sum produced the least fair rosters**. They also found that fairer rosters cost more total violations ([Martin et al.](https://people.cs.nott.ac.uk/pszeo/docs/publications/fairnessNurseRosteringCoop.pdf)). This matters because the CP-SAT objective is presumably a weighted sum. A fairness view built on the same per-person quantity checks something the solver does not guarantee. Unfairness has costs: Wolbeck's review links perceived unfair schedules to lower satisfaction, absenteeism and turnover ([Wolbeck 2019](https://d-nb.info/1202043291/34)). Kida and Takemura found that shift assignment affected turnover intention directly, independent of perceived justice ([Kida & Takemura](https://doi.org/10.1111/jjns.12570)).

Let x_i be person i's burden in a category (nights, Sundays, holidays, covers, overtime hours) over a window. Normalise it first, x′_i = x_i ÷ FTE_i (or ÷ contracted hours), so part-timers do not look favoured. Exclude people who were ineligible in the window: on leave, newly hired, or exempt from nights. The actionable per-person number is **d_i = x′_i − mean(x′)**, for example "Ana: +3 nights over the team average". The team summary is **MAD = (1/n) Σ |x′_i − mean(x′)|**, which reads in the same units as the data and is what MinDev minimises. For one scale-free trend number per category, use **CV = σ/mean** or **Gini G = Σ_i Σ_j |x′_i − x′_j| ÷ (2 n² mean)**, multiplied by n/(n−1) for small teams. Do not add Jain's index as well. It equals 1/(1+CV²) and so adds nothing ([Jain et al. 1984](https://research.engineering.wustl.edu/~jain/atmf/ftp/af_fairn.pdf)). A recent Turkish operating-room study of 37 nurses, a team close to this one in size, reported SD, CV, Gini and range side by side. Monthly working-day Gini fell from 0.096 to 0.007 under the model, while **nights still ranged from 1 to 8 per nurse** ([Demircioğlu & Mutlu 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13606332)). Equalising one dimension does not equalise the others, so report each category separately.

Rare events need longer windows. One or two holidays a month make single-month counts noisy. The long-term-fairness literature (Bard & Purnomo's growing penalty weights, accumulated satisfaction scores) carries counters across periods ([Wolbeck 2019](https://d-nb.info/1202043291/34)). Gross et al. showed that a monthly satisfaction indicator fed into future plans produced equal and stable satisfaction over a long horizon, where monthly-only planning kept disadvantaging the same people ([Gross et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30046927)). Use rolling 3, 6 and 12-month windows for nights, Sundays and holidays.

Request fulfilment is the second fairness axis. Wolbeck notes that maximising total fulfilment without a fairness term means "the same persons will always be affected by violations" ([Wolbeck 2019](https://d-nb.info/1202043291/34)). Define **FR_i = met_i ÷ requests_i**, always shown with n, since 1 of 1 is not 9 of 10. Define **WFR_i = Σ w_priority·met ÷ Σ w_priority**, and the cumulative **unmet-request debt_i = Σ w_priority·(1 − met)** over rolling months, which is the natural input for "who should be favoured next month". Report libre, quiero and evitar separately, and split out requests unmet because of hard constraints (coverage, legal rest) as "unmet for coverage". The evidence is directional. A 23-study review found the self-scheduling evidence base limited and mixed ([Wright et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33016472/)), and a 2024 review reported gains in autonomy and satisfaction ([O'Connell et al. 2024](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jocn.17114)).

Overtime concentration needs its own view, because totals hide who carries the load. In a 4-year study of 1,155 hospital workers, **more than 45 h/month of overtime was associated with high burnout (aOR 3.14)** ([Chen et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12345989/)). Among Korean nurses, **only mandatory overtime was significantly related to intent to leave** ([Bae 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11091319/)). The metrics are **top-N share = Σ_top4 OT_i ÷ Σ OT_i** (top 10% of 40 people, against an equal-share baseline of 10%), **share with any overtime = #(OT_i > 0) ÷ n**, and a per-person threshold flag. The 45 h figure comes from Taiwanese and Japanese practice. For Colombia, use the art. 168 CST limit of 2 h/day and 12 h/week ([Gerencie](https://www.gerencie.com/horas-extras-y-recargos-nocturnos-dominicales-y-festivos.html)) or local policy. If the change log records who started a cover, split voluntary from assigned overtime. The same Lorenz/Pareto component can serve the existing "who covers most" ranking.

The hours bank already exists, so reuse its balance B_i. Add the NHS framing on top. NHS England's e-rostering standards make **"planned versus delivered hours (net hours) per WTE"** a core KPI, with a target of **under 13 h per WTE**, reported monthly by team ([NHS England 2019](https://www.england.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/E-rostering_meaningful_use_standards.pdf)). The team KPI is mean(|B_i| ÷ FTE_i), plus the share of people with |B_i| above a threshold, shown as a sorted diverging bar per person. The 13 h is an NHS reference point, not a Colombian norm.

| Question | Chart | Window |
|---|---|---|
| Who is above or below the team? | Sorted diverging bar of x′_i − mean, ±1 MAD band | Rolling 3–12 months |
| Is it getting fairer? | One Gini or CV line per category by month | Monthly + rolling |
| Is overtime or covering concentrated? | Lorenz curve labelled "top 4 = X%", or a Pareto bar | Monthly |
| Are requests met fairly? | Bar of WFR_i labelled with n; team Gini of FR as a tile | Monthly |
| Is someone repeatedly unlucky? | Cumulative unmet-debt line per person, top 5 highlighted | Rolling |

With n ≈ 40, one or two people move any index. Always show the people (dot or strip plot, per-person bars) next to the index. No study has tested which fairness measure or chart staff perceive as fairest ([Wolbeck 2019](https://d-nb.info/1202043291/34)). The choice of MAD and per-person bars rests on their solver performance and on how easy they are to explain.

## Premium equivalent hours separate volume from Colombia's rate steps

With no salary data, express cost in hours. Define **premium equivalent hours PEH = Σ_h rate(h, date)** over all worked hours h, where rate is the surcharge fraction in force on that date. An 8 h Sunday shift now counts as 7.2 PEH. Ordinary hours + PEH gives full cost in hour units. The rate table must be dated, because the law changes in steps. The Sunday/holiday surcharge was **75% until 30 June 2025, 80% from 1 July 2025, 90% from 1 July 2026, and 100% from 1 July 2027** ([Bloomberg Línea](https://www.bloomberglinea.com/latinoamerica/colombia/pago-de-domingos-y-festivos-sube-al-90-desde-hoy-1-de-julio-de-2026-en-colombia/); [Buk](https://www.buk.co/blog/reforma-laboral-ley-2466-de-2025-impacto-en-nomina)). **Night work has run 19:00–06:00 since 25 December 2025** (previously from 21:00), and the night surcharge stays at 35% ([Actualícese](https://actualicese.com/recargo-nocturno-en-colombia-conoce-el-horario-aplicable-a-partir-del-25-de-diciembre-de-2025/)). The ordinary week fell to 42 h on 15 July 2026 ([Bloomberg Línea](https://www.bloomberglinea.com/latinoamerica/colombia/pago-de-domingos-y-festivos-sube-al-90-desde-hoy-1-de-julio-de-2026-en-colombia/)). Overtime is +25% by day and +75% by night ([Gerencie](https://www.gerencie.com/horas-extras-y-recargos-nocturnos-dominicales-y-festivos.html)). Secondary sources disagree on how surcharges stack for Sunday night overtime, so confirm that rule with a labour lawyer before relying on it. Check whether the app's rate table models the night-window change. If it does not, night hours and night counts before and after 25 December 2025 cannot be compared.

Two charts are enough. The first is **monthly stacked bars of PEH by component** (night, Sunday/holiday, daytime overtime, night overtime) with vertical markers at each legal step. It lets a reader see whether a rise came from scheduling more Sunday hours or from the new rate. To make that explicit, split the change into a volume effect (Δhours × old rate) and a rate effect (hours × Δrate). The second is **a 2027 projection**: re-price the last 12 months of Sunday/holiday hours at 90% and at 100%. The increase is Sunday hours × 0.10, so 300 Sunday hours a month adds 30 PEH a month. The rest of the existing data supports one more proxy, **excess cost = over-hours × surcharge rate of those slots**, which puts a rough price on overstaffing. Understaffing cannot be priced from the available data. Press coverage says the reform raises labour cost in health and forces a review of shift programming, but gives no figures ([Bloomberg Línea](https://www.bloomberglinea.com/latinoamerica/colombia/pago-de-domingos-y-festivos-sube-al-90-desde-hoy-1-de-julio-de-2026-en-colombia/)). No official projection method was found.

## Solver runs and the change log expose planning quality

The dashboard measures the roster but not the process that produced it. The process KPIs below need only solver runs, the change log and a publication timestamp. **Publication lead time** = roster period start − publication timestamp, in days, drawn as a bar per period with reference lines at 14 days and 6–8 weeks. The 14-day line comes from fair-workweek research: Seattle's secure-scheduling ordinance raised the share of workers getting at least two weeks' notice by 13 percentage points ([HKS](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/faculty-research/policy-topics/business-regulation/study-more-notice-shift-schedules-improves-lives)). The 6–8 week line is the practice of Australian hospital rosterers ([Booker et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11057102/)). NHS trust policies use 6–12 weeks, and an NHS England case study linked approval lead time to fill rate ([NHS England](https://www.england.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/Improving_workforce_visibility_through_e-rostering.pdf)). No Colombian legal minimum for roster notice was found.

From the solver log, compute the following per period. **Runs per published roster** is the number of runs between the previous publication and this one. **Feasibility rate** is runs with status OPTIMAL or FEASIBLE ÷ all runs. **Median solve time** is the median of end − start. Also track the **residual shortages and unmet requests of the run that was published**, trended across periods. A dot plot of runs per period, coloured by status, shows planning churn. For stability, extend what already exists in two ways. Normalise the change rate as **changes after publication per 100 shifts, split by type**: swaps are employee-driven, service-need changes are reactive, and adjustments are the manual-edit rate. Add a **published-vs-current diff**, the % of person-day assignments that differ between the two versions. Legion's dashboards, which show whether manager edits raised or lowered the schedule score, are the closest vendor analogue ([Legion](https://legion.co/resources/datasheets/automated-scheduling-datasheet/pdf)). No benchmark exists for feasibility rate, runs per roster or changes per 100 shifts, so these are trend-only metrics. "Time saved vs manual" cannot be measured without a recorded baseline. The literature context is 1–3+ days a month for manual rostering ([Booker et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11057102/)) and vendor-reported cuts of 50–90% ([HosPortal](https://www.hosportal.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/RHHAIJan2025v2.pdf)).

Absence replacement and seasonality close the loop. Define **replacement fill rate** = absence-affected shifts that were covered ÷ absence-affected shifts, plus **unfilled shifts per month** and **time-to-fill** = shift start − timestamp of the change-log entry that covered it. Vendor data suggest that shifts requested at least 6 weeks ahead fill about 10% better than those requested within a week ([Patchwork](https://www.patchwork.health/blog/patchwork-insights-5-tactics-to-improve-your-shift-fill-rates)). Healthcare sickness absence is seasonal, and an ambulance-service study found winter rises "largely predictable" with SARIMA ([BMJ Open 2021](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/185096/1/e053885.full.pdf)). A 40-person team with a short history does not justify such models. Show **absence-days by month and type** (planned vacation and leave split from unplanned sickness), one line per year, with a **seasonal-naive forecast** (same month last year) and a 3-month moving average as a band. Research uses these simple baselines as the benchmark that more complex models must beat ([IEEE DSAA 2024](https://unpaywall.org/10.1109%2FDSAA61799.2024.10722806)). Label the band "reference, not forecast" until there are two years of data. Two data gaps limit this section. Absences have no `created_at`, so true notice cannot be measured. Lead time needs a stored publication timestamp.

Do not build a Legion-style composite score. For one team, the separate components (coverage, unmet requests, rule violations) are easier to read and to explain to staff.

## Conclusion

The pattern across all three bodies of evidence is that aggregates mislead at this team's scale and grain. Daily totals hide slot-level gaps, team-wide indexes hide individuals at n ≈ 40, single months hide cumulative unfairness in rare shifts, and rostered hours hide the rate changes the law is introducing. The common fix is to compute at the finest grain the data allows (slot, person, dated rate), then aggregate with explicit windows and baselines that the UI names: per-person deviation from the FTE-normalised team mean, rolling 3 to 12 months, ineligible people excluded. A second point concerns the solver. CP-SAT optimises a weighted sum, and the fairness literature shows that weighted sums are the least fair objective. The dashboard's per-person fairness and unmet-debt views therefore audit the solver rather than duplicate it. The cumulative unmet-request debt is also a ready input if the model later adds long-term fairness weights.

The weakest links should shape how firmly the dashboard presents its numbers. Every threshold here is borrowed: the 15% shortfall, 90–110% RAG bands, 13 h per WTE, 45 h of overtime and 14 days' notice all come from NHS, nursing or US retail contexts, not from Colombian sterilisation or radiology services. They should be configurable, and labelled as reference lines rather than targets. The Ley 2466 dates and the surcharge stacking rules come from secondary sources and need checking against the official text. Two cheap schema changes, a `created_at` on absences and a stored publication timestamp, unlock most of the planning-process metrics. They are worth making before building charts that depend on them.
